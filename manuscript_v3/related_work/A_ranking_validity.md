### Ranking instability, benchmark validity and cross-dataset agreement

Medical image analysis has a substantial literature on how far method rankings can be trusted. Maier-Hein et al. (2018) analysed 150 biomedical image analysis challenges and showed that an algorithm's rank is not robust to changes in the test data, the ranking scheme or the observers who produce the reference annotations. Later work took up parts of this problem. Wiesenfarth et al. (2021) released challengeR, an open-source toolkit for analysing and visualising ranking stability and uncertainty, demonstrated on the Medical Segmentation Decathlon. The BIAS statement (Maier-Hein et al., 2020) set out a reporting checklist so that challenge results can be interpreted and reproduced. Metrics Reloaded (Maier-Hein et al., 2024) and its companion paper on metric pitfalls (Reinke et al., 2024) showed that metrics are often chosen without regard to the domain goal and gave a structured process for choosing them. These works mostly treat the ranking produced by one challenge test set as the object of study, and ask how stable it is under resampling, aggregation and metric choice. Our question is different: whether two independently collected clinical reference sets rank the same methods in the same order. Agreement within one dataset is high in our data (about 0.94), which fits the picture of fairly stable single-dataset rankings, but it falls to about 0.5 between independent datasets of one modality. Resampling a single test set cannot reveal this gap, because it never includes the change of patients, devices and annotation protocol that separates two datasets.

A second strand asks whether reported improvements survive a fair comparison. nnU-Net (Isensee et al., 2021) configured itself automatically and outperformed most existing approaches on 23 public datasets, including solutions designed for individual datasets. nnU-Net Revisited (Isensee et al., 2024) found that many claimed gains of newer architectures over U-Net baselines did not hold once weak baselines, insufficient datasets and unequal compute were accounted for. Varoquaux and Cheplygina (2022) described how biased data and publication incentives distort evaluation in medical imaging. Eisenmann et al. (2023) analysed the 80 competitions held at ISBI 2021 and MICCAI 2021 and reported that winners commonly used multi-task learning and multi-stage pipelines, focused on augmentation, preprocessing, data curation and postprocessing, and designed their methods around the challenge metric. The Medical Segmentation Decathlon (Antonelli et al., 2022) tested whether a method that performs well across many tasks generalises to an unseen task, and concluded that consistent multi-task performance is a strong surrogate for generalisability. These studies ask which methods are best and why, and they compare trained networks retrained on each task. Our pool deliberately mixes 21 classical algorithms, 3 pixel classifiers and 11 trained networks, and the composition of that pool changes the answer. A synthetic benchmark reaches 98% of the clinical-to-clinical ceiling over 22 mostly classical methods but 49% over all 32. When trained and untrained methods are mixed, a cross-domain rank comparison partly measures how well each method's training domain matches the test domain, an effect that a pool of networks retrained on every task would not show.

General machine learning has studied rank transfer across distribution shifts directly. Recht et al. (2019) rebuilt test sets for CIFAR-10 and ImageNet by replicating the original collection process; accuracy dropped by about 3% to 15% and 11% to 14% respectively, yet gains on the original test sets carried over to the new ones. Miller et al. (2021) showed that out-of-distribution accuracy is strongly correlated with in-distribution accuracy across many models and shifts, with weaker correlation for some synthetic CIFAR-10-C shifts and for Camelyon17-WILDS tissue classification. Baek et al. (2022) found a matching linear relation for agreement between pairs of classifiers and reported that it appears to hold only for neural networks. Taori et al. (2020) evaluated 204 ImageNet models and found that robustness to synthetic perturbations transferred little or not at all to natural shifts. Dehghani et al. (2021) showed that changing the set of benchmark tasks can change which algorithms appear best. Two features separate these results from ours. First, they use a fixed zoo of mostly neural classifiers on natural images, so rank preservation is measured within a fairly homogeneous pool. Second, the shifted test sets are often constructed to stay close to the original distribution. Our references are independently collected clinical datasets, and our pool spans methods with and without training. The exceptions noted by Miller et al. (2021) and Baek et al. (2022), a medical dataset and non-neural models, describe the conditions our study sits in. Our results suggest that in this setting rank agreement is a property of a benchmark together with its method pool, not of the benchmark alone.
